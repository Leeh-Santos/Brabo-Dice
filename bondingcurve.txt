Token starts on a mathematical curve, not a pool. Price is a pure function of supply sold.
Zero slippage — the curve is the price. No reserves to imbalance.
Every buy adds ETH to a reserve. The reserve grows as people buy.
At a threshold (~$69k mcap on pump.fun), the accumulated ETH plus a token allocation auto-seeds a real Uniswap/Raydium pool, and the LP gets burned.

The lifecycle

Phase 1 — curve. Total supply is fixed at deploy, say 1B tokens, split into two buckets: ~800M sellable on the curve, ~200M reserved for the future LP. Buyers send ETH, the contract prices from f(totalSold) and delivers tokens. ETH piles up in the contract. No pool exists yet.

Phase 2 — graduation. A threshold trips (pump.fun uses ~$69k market cap, which lands when the curve has sold its allocation and accumulated ~85 SOL). The contract then, in one transaction:

Takes the accumulated ETH
Takes the reserved 200M tokens
Creates the Uniswap pool with both
Burns the LP NFT

That last step is the point. Burning makes the liquidity permanently unremovable — nobody can rug it, not even the deployer. It's the credibility guarantee the whole model runs on.

Phase 3. Curve is dead, pool is live, normal token from here.

Two things worth knowing

The initial pool price must equal the final curve price. If the curve ends at 0.0001 ETH/token, you seed the pool at exactly that ratio — otherwise the first trader arbitrages the gap. So the reserved token count isn't arbitrary; it's derived from accumulated_ETH ÷ final_curve_price.

Some curves let you sell back. Pump.fun does — you can exit to the curve before graduation, at a slight discount. That gives buyers an exit before there's a pool, which matters for trust. Optional, but it's why people are willing to buy at token #1.



3 types of onding 

Linear — price = m × totalSold + b

Simplest to reason about, easy to explain to buyers, but the price ratio from first to last buyer is modest, so there's less reward for showing up early.

Exponential — price = b × e^(k × totalSold)

Steep late-stage pricing, strong early-buyer advantage. Harder to implement safely in Solidity (needs a fixed-point exp — PRBMath or solmate's wadExp) and overflow-prone if you get the scaling wrong.

Constant product — price = k / (supply_remaining)², the pump.fun approach

Uses virtual reserves: x · y = k where x and y are bookkeeping numbers rather than deposited assets. Cheap to compute, well-tested, and it graduates into a Uniswap pool with matching math, so the price curve is continuous across the transition. That last property is why pump.fun uses it.
